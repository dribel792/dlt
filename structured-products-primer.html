0Building blocks you need first

Almost every structured product is built from a few parts. Learn these five and the rest of the doc is just combinations.

Block 1
Call option

The right to buy an asset at a fixed price (the strike) on a set date (the expiry). You pay a premium for that right.

Selling a call means you collect the premium today. In return you give up any gain above the strike: if the price ends above it, you pay the difference.

Block 2
Put option

The right to sell an asset at the strike. If you buy a put, you have a price floor: when the price falls below the strike, the put pays the difference.

It works like an insurance policy on the price. The premium is what the policy costs.

Block 3
Collar

Buy a put (floor) and sell a call (cap) on the same asset. The call premium you collect pays for the put. If the call is worth more than the put, the collar earns you money (a net credit).

Result: the value of the asset is held inside a range. You give up gains above the cap and are protected below the floor.

Block 4
Binary (digital) contract

Pays a fixed amount (e.g. $1) if an event happens and $0 if it doesn't. Prediction markets trade these. A price of $0.30 means the market thinks the event has a ~30% chance.

Block 5
Note / certificate with an ISIN

A security (legally a debt instrument of the issuer) whose payout is defined by a formula, e.g. "pays the value of this vault" or "pays this basket". The ISIN is its ID number. Because it has one, any bank custody account can hold it, settle it through Clearstream and report on it.

Three risks to always ask about
Market · counterparty · basis
  • Market risk: the price moves against you.
  • Counterparty risk: whoever owes you money (e.g. the party on the other side of your option) fails to pay.
  • Basis risk: your hedge is on a slightly different thing from what you hold, so the two don't move exactly together.

1Collared loan (ZiC-style): how it works

The client borrows stablecoins against BTC at 0% interest with no liquidation before maturity. In exchange, the client agrees to a price range. Below the floor the collateral is sold at the floor price; above the cap it is sold at the cap price.

This isn't new. Private banks have sold the same thing to wealthy clients with large single-stock positions for decades, as "collar loans" or "equity monetisation". What's new is offering it to ordinary clients on crypto and tokenized assets.

The mechanics

1 · ClientLocks 0.1 BTC ($10,000 at $100k) and receives a $5,000 USDC loan (50% LTV, 6 months, 0%)
→
2 · Issuer / hedge deskBuys a put at $70k (floor) and sells a call at $140k (cap) on 0.1 BTC
→
3 · PremiumCall premium − put premium = net credit. It pays the cost of funding the $5,000
→
4 · MaturityThe BTC price is compared with the range and one of three outcomes applies

Worked example: the three outcomes

Setup: 0.1 BTC collateral at $100k = $10,000 · loan $5,000 · floor (put strike) $70k · cap (call strike) $140k · 6 months.

BTC at maturityWhat the options doValue availableLoan repaidClient receives
$90,000 (in range)Both options expire worthless0.1 BTC = $9,000$5,000 (in USDC, or by selling 0.05556 BTC)0.1 BTC back if repaid in USDC, or 0.04444 BTC ($4,000)
$55,000 (below floor)Put pays 0.1 × ($70k − $55k) = $1,500BTC sold at $5,500 + put $1,500 = $7,000$5,000$2,000
$180,000 (above cap)Short call costs 0.1 × ($180k − $140k) = $4,000BTC $18,000 − call $4,000 = $14,000$5,000$9,000 (gives up $4,000 of upside vs. no cap)
Design pitfall: pay the leftover in USDC (or in BTC at spot), never in BTC priced at the strike The options protect a dollar value. Paying the leftover as a BTC amount calculated at the strike price breaks the hedge:
  • At $55k: $2,000 ÷ $70k = 0.02857 BTC, which is worth only $1,571 at spot. The client gets less than the floor promised.
  • At $180k: $9,000 ÷ $140k = 0.06429 BTC, which is worth $11,571 at spot. The issuer pays out $2,571 more than the hedge covers, on every such deal.
The fix is to pay $2,000 / $9,000 in USDC, or the BTC equivalent at the spot price (0.03636 / 0.05 BTC). Also, Deribit options are cash-settled: the desk sells the collateral BTC at spot and receives the option payout separately. Nothing is "delivered to the put counterparty".

Where the "0%" actually comes from

Someone has to fund $5,000 for 6 months. At a funding cost of ~4% a year that's about $100. The collar pays for it: the premium from the $140k call must cover the $70k put plus the ~$100 funding plus our margin. If it doesn't, the desk adjusts the terms:

  • Lower the cap, e.g. $130k. The call is more valuable, so we collect more premium.
  • Lower the floor, e.g. $65k. The put is cheaper.
  • Lower the LTV, so there is less to fund.

The client still sees "0% interest". The cost is hidden in the range they accept. That is the whole structuring trick: turn an interest rate into an option trade the client finds easier to accept.

Note on numbersOption premiums depend on volatility, skew and rates on the day. Numbers here show how the structure works; they are not a pricing quote.

2Funding at scale: the collared-lending vault

The problem: If the issuer funds every loan from its own balance sheet, growth stops at the size of that balance sheet. $50m of loans needs $50m of our own capital.

The solution: Outside lenders deposit USDC into a pool (a "vault"). The vault funds the loans, and the lenders are paid from the collar net credit. "Morpho-style" just means the onchain version of this: a smart-contract vault with transparent rules, where anyone can check every loan, its collateral and its hedge.

LendersDeposit USDC (funds, treasuries, other platforms)
→
VaultLends USDC to clients against BTC/ETH/tokenized stocks, max 50% LTV
→
Hedge deskPuts a collar on every loan. The net credit flows back to the vault as interest
→
LendersEarn yield that comes from option premium, not from the borrower

Is it riskless for the lender?

Almost, as far as price goes. Even in a crash the floor protects the loan:

Stress test: BTC −45% (from $100k to $55k) Collateral sold at spot = $5,500 + put payout $1,500 = $7,000 against a $5,000 loan → the lender is covered 1.4×. The loan is only at risk if BTC falls below ~$50k and the put doesn't pay. So the remaining risk is about whether the hedge pays, not about the price.

That is what "safer yield" means here: safer than a normal overcollateralised DeFi loan (Aave/Morpho). There the lender relies on liquidating in time, and a fast crash or an oracle failure can leave bad debt. Here a bought option sets the floor. What remains:

Remaining riskWhat it meansMitigation
Hedge counterpartyDeribit or the OTC counterparty fails to pay the putSpread hedges across venues, use cleared venues, set counterparty limits
CustodyCollateral lost or frozen at the custodianRegulated custodian, segregated wallets, proof of reserves

Bottom lineThe lender's position works like a senior secured loan with a bought put attached. The yield should end up somewhere around "T-bills plus a spread", and the spread pays for counterparty and operational risk, not for market risk. That makes it a natural fit for conservative money (§4–§5).

3Diversified portfolios, lombard and why 24/7 matters

Is a balanced portfolio safer collateral than a single asset?

Yes. A mix of stocks, bonds, money-market funds and some BTC swings far less than BTC alone, so a lender can lend more against it. The catch is hedging. Nobody lists options on your specific portfolio, so you have three choices:

Option A
Haircut only (classic lombard)

Lend a % of each asset's value (e.g. high for government bonds and money-market funds, medium for blue-chip stocks, low for single crypto). No options. If the value falls, margin call, then liquidation.

Cheap, standard, but liquidation risk is back.

Option B
Collar every holding

Buy a put and sell a call on each stock and coin separately. Precise, but expensive: individual puts together cost more than one put on the whole basket, because you pay for crashes that never happen at the same time.

Option C
Collar with index options

Hedge the stock part with options on an index (e.g. Euro Stoxx 50) and BTC with BTC options. Cheap, but basis risk: the client's stocks may fall while the index doesn't.

Practical split Diversified portfolio → classic lombard with haircuts (Option A).
Concentrated position (BTC, ETH, one big stock) → collared loan (§1).
Both can sit in the same app. The client posts their whole portfolio, and the engine picks which treatment applies to each part.

Why 24/7 matters (and where it doesn't)

  • For collared loans it hardly matters. Settlement happens only at maturity, so nothing needs to happen on a Sunday.
  • For lombard on mixed collateral it matters a lot. Example: on Saturday BTC drops 15%. The stock part of the portfolio is still priced at Friday's close, and the stock market can't be traded until Monday. The lender is blind and can't act for two days, so traditional lenders apply bigger haircuts to cover the gap.
    If the stocks are tokenized and trade 24/7, they can be valued and sold at any time. That means tighter haircuts, higher LTV and less weekend gap risk.
  • Client access. The client can borrow at any time, e.g. Saturday night when they need liquidity. This is a selling point, not the core economics.

4Tranching: "insurance" without being an insurer

Why not just sell insurance on vault yield? Insurance is limited by capital. To insure $100m you need a lot of money on standby, an insurance licence (Solvency II in the EU), and the losses all arrive together: when a stablecoin depegs, every policy pays out at once.

Tranching gets the same result without us putting up the capital. Split one pool into two slices with different rights:

  • Senior tranche: paid first, gets a fixed, lower return, and loses money last.
  • Junior tranche: paid last, gets whatever is left over (higher return), and takes losses first.

The junior investors are the insurers, and the yield the senior gives up pays them. The order in which money flows out is called the waterfall.

Worked example

Vault $100m earning 8% a year. Senior $80m promised 5%. Junior $20m gets the rest.

ScenarioVault resultSenior ($80m)Junior ($20m)
Good year+$8m income, no losses+$4m → +5%+$4m → +20%
Weak year+$5m income+$4m → +5%+$1m → +5%
Loss year+$8m income, −$10m loss+$4m → +5% (untouched)20 + 4 − 10 = $14m → −30%
Disaster+$8m income, −$30m lossEnds at $78m → −2.5%Wiped out → −100%

The senior only loses money once losses exceed the junior's capital plus the junior's income, about $24m (24% of the vault) in this example. That cushion is called subordination, and the loss level at which the senior starts to lose is the attachment point.

Combine with §2Tranche the collared-lending vault. Its losses are already small because every loan is hedged, so a senior tranche with 15–20% subordination underneath it is very conservative. That senior tranche is the product to wrap for corporate treasuries and banks (§5). The junior goes to crypto-native funds that want higher yield.
The 2007 lessonTranching does not reduce total risk. It only moves it around. The senior is only as safe as the loss estimates behind it. CDOs blew up because everyone assumed mortgages wouldn't all default together, and in a crisis they did. Size the junior against a real stress scenario (e.g. hedge counterparty fails and BTC −60% in a week), not the average year, and publish the assumptions.

5Wrapping it in an ISIN

The core point: institutions don't avoid onchain yield because the yield is bad. They avoid it because they can't hold it. A corporate treasurer or a small bank has:

  • a custody account at their bank that holds securities, not wallets;
  • an investment policy that allows "bonds / notes / funds", not "smart-contract tokens";
  • accounting and auditors who need a known instrument type, prices and statements;
  • settlement through Clearstream/Euroclear.

A note with an ISIN solves all four at once. The yield engine stays onchain; the client only sees a security in their custody account.

So: do we wrap looped yield in an ISIN?

Yes, the wrapper is the product, but put something conservative inside. Raw leveraged DeFi loops carry depeg, oracle and curator risk that a treasury mandate won't accept, whatever the wrapper. The best thing to put inside is the senior tranche of the collared-lending vault (§2 + §4), optionally mixed with tokenized money-market funds. That gives a story a treasurer can sign off: "T-bill-plus yield, secured by hedged collateral, in a normal security."

What the investor is actually exposed to

  • Issuer risk. A note is the issuer's debt. If the issuer fails, holders are creditors. Fix: issue from a bankruptcy-remote SPV and pledge the underlying assets to a security trustee, the way physically backed ETCs work. Then holders have a claim on the assets, not just on the issuer.
  • The strategy inside, i.e. everything in §2 and §4.
  • Liquidity of the note: whether someone quotes a bid every day. We do, as market maker.

Paperwork (high level, verify with Legal)

  • Base prospectus + final terms, approved by BaFin and passported to other EU countries. This is the same machinery as our certificate programme.
  • PRIIPs KID if the note is sold to retail clients.
  • MiFID target market: who it is for and who it is not for.
  • If the vault lends, check whether that is lending business under the KWG (banking licence) and how the note/SPV structure handles it (§8).

6Prediction-market baskets

Decision (Max, 17 Nov 2026)The bets are baskets containing dozens of hedges, each clearly auditable, and we market-make them ourselves. Liquidity and market making are not the constraint. This section explains how the product works on that basis.

From single bet to basket

A single binary ("Will X happen by date Y?") is a blunt hedge. It pays everything or nothing, and depends on one specific wording. A basket bundles 10–30 binaries that all point in the same direction (e.g. "AI development slows down"). Each leg gets a weight. The basket pays the weighted sum of the legs that resolve "yes".

Payoff formula (linear basket)Payout per 100 notional = Σ (weighti × outcomei) where outcome = 1 if the event happened and 0 if not.
Fair price per 100 notional = Σ (weighti × probabilityi), with probabilities taken from where each leg trades.

Worked example: "AI slowdown" basket (illustrative legs and probabilities)

#Leg (resolves YES if…)WeightImplied prob.Price contribution
1GPU rental price index (H100/hr) below $1.50 by 30 Jun 202715%30%4.50
2NVIDIA data-centre revenue growth < 20% YoY in a reported quarter by Jun 202715%20%3.00
3≥ 2 of the top-4 hyperscalers cut capex guidance in H1 202715%15%2.25
4A top-5 AI lab raises at a lower valuation (down round) by Jun 202710%12%1.20
5US tightens AI chip export rules (new BIS rule) by Jun 202710%45%4.50
6EU delays enforcement of AI Act general-purpose-AI obligations10%35%3.50
7SOX semiconductor index down > 25% from basket start15%18%2.70
8A ≥ 1 GW US data-centre project announced as cancelled or paused10%40%4.00
Basket100%25.65
How it plays out The buyer pays ~25.65 per 100 notional (plus our spread). Say legs 1, 3 and 7 come true: payout = 15 + 15 + 15 = 45 per 100, a gain of ~19 per 100. If nothing happens, the buyer loses the premium, exactly like an insurance premium. If the whole theme plays out, the payout approaches 100.

Why baskets beat single bets

  • Smoother hedge: it pays partly when the theme partly happens, instead of all-or-nothing on one wording.
  • Less wording risk: one leg resolving on a technicality only moves the basket by its weight.
  • Auditable: each leg has a fixed resolution source, date and rule, written into the final terms. An independent calculation agent confirms the outcomes.
  • Packaging: one ISIN, one price, one line on the client's statement, instead of 20 positions across venues.

Basket designs

Default
Linear (weighted sum)

The payout is the weighted sum of the legs that came true. Each leg is priced and hedged on its own, so it is the simplest to quote, market-make and audit.

Advanced
"k-of-n" trigger

Pays 100 if at least k of the n legs happen, otherwise 0. It's closer to a classic insurance trigger, but the price depends on how correlated the legs are, so it is harder to price and hedge. Phase 2.

How we market-make it

  1. Quote: basket mid = Σ weight × leg mid, using live prices from Polymarket, Kalshi and our own book. Bid/ask = mid ± spread.
  2. Hedge: when a client buys the basket, we buy the matching weight of each leg on the venues, or warehouse legs where the flow from different clients offsets.
  3. Manage the remainder: leftover exposure from legs we can't fully hedge, and correlation between legs, sits in the market-making book under risk limits.
  4. Publish: leg list, weights, resolution sources and a daily indicative value, so anyone can rebuild the price.

Example: sizing a hedge for an exporter A German machine builder sells €20m a year into a region. In an escalation scenario, management estimates revenue there falls by ~30%, a €6m hit.
  1. Pick or build a "regional escalation" basket (tariffs, sanctions, shipping-lane disruption, export-control legs…).
  2. Estimate how much the basket pays in the scenario they fear. Say ~60 per 100, because most but not all legs trigger.
  3. Notional needed ≈ €6m ÷ 0.60 = €10m.
  4. Cost = basket price × notional. At a price of 8 per 100 that is €800k, the "insurance premium" for €6m of protection.
The CFO now has a number to compare with alternatives (trade-credit insurance, export guarantees, simply holding more cash). The basket design should map as tightly as possible onto how that company actually loses money.
Verify with LegalIn the EU, binaries are derivatives. Sold to retail clients there are ESMA/BaFin product-intervention rules for binary options, and we need to check whether a securitised, prospectus-backed basket note falls inside or outside them. For institutions: MiFID II, and EMIR if traded OTC. Corporates and IFRS 9 hedge accounting: the buyer needs to document the economic link between the basket and their exposure.

The Leonteq model, applied to onchain

Leonteq (CH) built a business by letting partner banks issue structured products under the bank's own name. The bank owns the client relationship and the shelf, and Leonteq does structuring, pricing, hedging, issuance and lifecycle in the background. Banks pay for this because building a structuring desk themselves is slow and expensive.

The onchain version is even more compelling for a bank, because it can't hedge against Polymarket, Deribit or Morpho liquidity itself, not operationally and not under its risk framework. We can. So what we sell to a bank is:

Sell 1
Collateral

Lombard and collared loans on tokenized assets and crypto for the bank's clients, valued 24/7.

Sell 2
Shelf products

Thematic prediction baskets, collared notes and yield notes under the bank's brand.

Sell 3
Treasury yield

The senior-tranche yield note for the bank's own treasury and its corporate clients.

Why a bank says yes (the Kraken × Deutsche Börse logic)Not because of day-one revenue. It's because the bank gets access to a market it can't build into, through a regulated EU counterparty, in instruments its systems already handle. For us the value is distribution and credibility, even before volume arrives.
