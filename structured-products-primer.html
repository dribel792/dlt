Tokenization and 24/7 markets: which financial products it unlocks

The products in this doc all exist today: lombard loans, securitizations, yield notes, collar loans, event hedges. They work, but they are slow, expensive, locked to one institution and closed at weekends. Tokenization and 24/7 markets don't change what these products pay. They change the plumbing underneath, and for several of them that turns a niche product into a mass-market one. This doc explains what changes, then goes through the products from the largest market to the smallest: for each one, who wants it, why today's version is inefficient, how it works onchain, and who we sell it to.

What tokenization and 24/7 markets actually change

A loan, a collar or a tranche pays out the same whether it is recorded on a blockchain or in a bank's core system. What changes is how cheaply, how quickly and how widely it can be produced and traded. Four things:

Unlock 1
Collateral can move

Today an asset can only be borrowed against at the institution that holds it. A tokenized asset can be pledged where it sits to any lender, so lenders compete for it. You don't have to move the account.

Unlock 2
Markets never close

Prices, margin calls and sales run 24/7. Lenders don't have to cover a weekend in which they can't act, so haircuts get smaller and LTVs higher. Hedges can be adjusted at any time, so more assets become hedgeable.

Unlock 3
Fixed costs fall

Payment order (the waterfall), settlement and reporting become code instead of trustees, paying agents and monthly PDFs. Deals that were too small to be worth doing become viable.

Unlock 4
Settlement is atomic

Asset and cash swap in one step. When a pledged asset is sold, the sale, the loan repayment and the release of the pledge happen in a single transaction, so neither side is exposed while it settles.

The core idea: every position becomes a token
A loan, a lender's claim, a tranche or a hedge is today an entry in one institution's ledger, stuck there until maturity. As a token it can be sold early, pledged as collateral, sliced into tranches and settled atomically.
  • Example: a lender funds a $1m, 12-month loan at 5%. After 3 months they need the cash. Today they wait 9 more months. With a loan token, they sell it to a fund for ~$1.01m (principal plus interest earned so far, minus a small discount). The borrower notices nothing; only the name of the creditor changes.
  • Why it matters: lenders who can exit early accept lower yields and longer terms, so borrowers get cheaper money. A secondary market turns a private loan book into an investable asset class. That is the thread running through every product below.

1Securities-backed lending: from lombard to a universal credit line

Market & demand

Borrowing against your investments instead of selling them is one of the largest and lowest-loss lending businesses in finance. US margin debt alone passed $1 trillion in 2025, and European private banks run large lombard books. Demand comes from people and companies who want liquidity without selling. They may not want to trigger tax, give up a position, or wait for cash. Typical uses are buying property, bridging a business payment, or paying taxes.

How a normal lombard loan works today

  1. The client's portfolio sits in a custody account at their bank and is pledged to that bank.
  2. The bank gives each holding a lending value (Beleihungswert), a percentage of its market value. Illustrative: government bonds 80–90%, diversified funds and ETFs 60–70%, blue-chip stocks 50–70%, small caps 0–40%. Crypto is usually 0%.
  3. The sum of the lending values is the credit limit. The client draws any amount up to it and pays interest only on what is drawn.
  4. The bank revalues the portfolio daily. If the drawn amount exceeds the limit, the client gets a margin call: add assets or repay. If not, the bank sells holdings.

Why it is inefficient today

  • Locked to the custodian. You can only borrow from the bank that holds your assets. Moving the depot to a cheaper lender takes days to weeks, so nobody does it. The custodian has a monopoly on your loan.
  • Closed at weekends and at night. The bank can't sell between Friday close and Monday open, so it sets wide haircuts to cover that gap.
  • Narrow collateral. Crypto, gold tokens and assets held at another institution count for nothing.
  • Exclusive. Private banks often start at €250k+; most neobrokers and small banks offer no lombard at all or only at poor LTVs.

Now: the universal credit line

One credit line against everything the client owns in tokenized form (tokenized money-market funds, bonds, ETFs and stocks, gold, crypto), pledged where it sits and valued 24/7.

  • Pledge in place: the pledge is recorded on the token. The client keeps ownership, dividends and votes. Any lender can see the collateral live and quote, so lenders compete.
  • Pledged ≠ frozen: the client can still sell. The buyer's payment repays the loan, releases the pledge and pays the rest to the client, all in one atomic step (sell-to-repay). They can also swap collateral (e.g. ETH out, T-bill fund in) as long as the LTV stays in limits. The only thing they can't do is move the asset to another wallet or broker while the loan is open.
  • 24/7 risk: margin calls and partial sales happen when the market moves, including Saturday, so haircuts can be tighter. The engine widens haircuts on assets whose home market is closed. A tokenized stock trades thinly on a Sunday, so the 24/7 gain is full for crypto, MMFs and gold, and partial for stocks.
  • Concentrated positions (e.g. 40% in BTC or one stock) get a collar instead of a haircut (§4), so the client gets no liquidation and a floor and cap instead.
Worked example Client holds €40k tokenized MMF, €30k tokenized ETFs, €20k BTC, €10k tokenized gold = €100k.
Limit = 40×90% + 30×65% + 20×50% + 10×75% = €73k. A classic lombard would ignore the BTC and the gold token, and would typically be at another bank anyway.
The client draws €40k. On Saturday BTC falls 20%: the limit drops by €2k to €71k, so nothing happens. Had they drawn the full €73k, the engine would sell ~€4k of BTC on Saturday instead of waiting blind until Monday.

What becomes a token

The collateral (pledged in place) and the loan itself. Pools of credit-line loans can be funded by a lender vault, sold as loan tokens, and tranched (§2), with the senior slice wrapped for institutions (§3). Funding in stages: partner-bank balance sheet → lender vault → senior tranche sold to institutions.

2Securitization & tranching: card receivables as the use case

The problem first

A card issuer (or any lender) has $100m of loans on its balance sheet. It has two problems:

  1. Its money is tied up. The $100m is lent out and comes back slowly. To lend more, it needs new funding, and as a smaller lender it can't get it cheaply. Today it borrows on an expensive bank warehouse line.
  2. Investors who could fund it want very different things. A pension fund or bank treasury wants a steady 5% and almost no chance of loss. A hedge fund wants 15% and can take losses. One undivided pool of card loans suits neither.

Securitization solves problem 1: the lender sells the loans to a separate vehicle, the vehicle issues notes to investors, the lender gets cash and lends again. Tranching solves problem 2: the vehicle issues several slices that take losses in a fixed order, so each investor buys the risk they want.

Why it is inefficient today

  • High fixed costs (~€1–1.5m upfront plus running costs, see above) mean only large issuers securitize. Fintechs, BNPL providers, neobanks and small banks stay on warehouse lines.
  • Opaque: investors get a monthly servicer report, so they demand a higher yield for what they can't see.
  • Illiquid: smaller tranches barely trade, so investors are stuck until maturity.

How tranching works (in short)

Picture a stack of champagne glasses. Income fills the senior glass first. Losses empty the junior glass first; only when it is empty does the mezzanine lose, and only then the senior. Same loans, but the slices are hit in a different order.

Slice of $100m card poolSizeCoupon (illustr.)Loses money when…Natural buyer
Senior$75m~5%losses exceed $25mBank treasuries, insurers, pension funds
Mezzanine$15m~8%losses exceed $10mCredit funds, asset managers

Why card pools work well: excess spread Card interest and fees ~22% − losses ~4–5% − investor coupons ~5–6% − servicing ~2% = ~9–11% excess spread per year, the first cushion before any tranche loses principal. If losses beyond that reach 6% ($6m), the junior loses 60% of its slice and the mezzanine and senior lose nothing. Card balances revolve, so the vehicle is a master trust: during a revolving period repayments buy new receivables. An early-amortization trigger pays investors down early if excess spread falls towards zero.
The 2007 lessonTranching moves risk around; it doesn't reduce it. The senior is only as safe as the loss assumptions behind it. Size the junior against a real stress scenario and publish the assumptions.

Now: securitization as a factory

  • The waterfall and triggers are code. Payment order, early amortization and reporting run onchain, which brings €10–50m deals into reach.
  • Loan-level transparency: delinquencies, payments and excess spread are visible daily. Investors should demand a lower yield for that.
  • Every tranche is a token: it can be sold before maturity, pledged as collateral (e.g. into the credit line, §1) or wrapped in an ISIN (§3).

Use case: US card debt, tokenized for EU investors

US card receivables yield more than European assets of similar risk, and the pool is deep. Packaging senior and mezzanine slices for EU investors is a natural cross-border product. The gate is the EU Securitisation Regulation (verify with Legal):

  • Risk retention: EU institutions may only buy if the originator keeps at least 5% of the risk. Many US deals don't, so we need a US originator who will.
  • Disclosure and due diligence to EU standards. Onchain loan-level data makes this much easier.
  • Retail is effectively closed (suitability test, allocation caps, minimum tickets), so this is a professional-investor product.
  • Other issues: the USD→EUR hedge costs part of the yield pick-up; servicing and collections stay under US consumer law; and buyers depend on one originator's underwriting. The EU's 2025 reform proposal loosens some rules but keeps risk retention.

Proof it works onchain: Figure originated and securitized billions in home-equity loans on its own blockchain (Provenance) and went public in 2025 on that model.

3Bank-ready yield notes: wrapping onchain yield in an ISIN

Market & demand

Corporate treasurers, small banks and foundations hold large cash balances and want "T-bills plus a little". Onchain there is attractive, well-secured yield: senior tranches (§2), hedged lending (§4) and tokenized money-market funds.

Why it is inefficient today

The yield isn't the problem. Institutions can't hold it. They have a custody account that holds securities, not a wallet. Their investment policy says "bonds, notes, funds", not "tokens". Auditors need a known instrument type, and settlement runs through Clearstream/Euroclear. So the money stays in deposits at a lower yield.

Now: the note is the bridge

A note with an ISIN, issued by a bankruptcy-remote SPV with the underlying assets pledged to a security trustee, the way physically backed ETCs work. The yield engine stays onchain; the client sees a normal security in their custody account. Wrapper types: ETN/ETP (rules-based, listed), AMC (a manager can rebalance), or a German eWpG crypto security (sits in a custody account and onchain at the same time).

What goes inside matters more than the wrapper: the senior tranche of a hedged lending pool or a card securitization, optionally mixed with tokenized MMFs. Raw leveraged DeFi loops are not suitable: no treasury mandate accepts their risks, whatever the wrapper. The pitch a treasurer can sign off on: "T-bill-plus yield, secured by hedged collateral, in a normal security."

Unlike the other products, tokenization gains nothing here by design. The wrapper exists because tokenization is still incomplete, and its role shrinks as institutional onchain custody grows. For the next years, though, it is the distribution channel for everything else.

Target clients

  • Corporate & SME treasurers: buy it through their house bank like any other note.
  • Small & regional banks: for their own investment book (Depot A) and to put on the shelf for their clients.
  • Big banks: white-label notes under their own name; we structure, hedge and make the market.
  • Foundations, family offices, insurers.
Watch-outs (verify with Legal)Base prospectus + final terms (BaFin, passported), PRIIPs KID if sold to retail, MiFID target market. Issuer and SPV structure and the security trustee have to be defined.

4Collared loans (ZiC) on anything that can be locked

Market & demand

Holders of a large single position (founders, early employees, crypto holders, companies with a BTC treasury) want cash without selling and without being liquidated in a crash. Crypto-backed lending is a market in the tens of billions. For stocks, private banks have sold the same product for decades as "collar loans" or "equity monetisation" (in the US often as a prepaid variable forward).

How it works (in short)

The client locks e.g. 0.1 BTC ($10,000) and gets $5,000 at 0% interest, with no liquidation for 6 months. The hedge desk buys a put at $70k (the floor) and sells a call at $140k (the cap). The call premium pays for the put and for the cost of funding the loan, and that is where the "0%" comes from. At maturity:

  • BTC in range: the client repays and gets the BTC back.
  • BTC at $55k: the put pays out. Collateral plus put = $7,000, so the loan is repaid and the client gets $2,000.
  • BTC at $180k: the client gives up the gain above $140k and gets $9,000.

Always pay the residual in USDC or in BTC at spot, never in BTC priced at the strike. Otherwise the hedge no longer matches what is owed.

Why it is inefficient today

  • Bespoke and exclusive: private banks only do it for multi-million single-stock positions, with OTC options and weeks of paperwork.
  • Limited to assets with a listed option market during exchange hours.
  • Funding is stuck: the lender's claim sits on the issuer's ledger until maturity, so growth is capped by the issuer's own balance sheet.

Now (1): structure it on anything that can be locked

Because markets are becoming 24/7, the product no longer needs a stock exchange. It works on any asset that meets three conditions:

  1. It can be locked (pledged as a token).
  2. It has a reliable 24/7 price.
  3. There is an option market on it, or something close enough to hedge with. 24/7 perps let the desk hedge its option risk at any hour, including weekends.

That covers BTC and ETH today, plus tokenized gold, tokenized indices and ETFs, single stocks as their tokenized and perp markets deepen, and over time FX and commodities. Every new 24/7 market is a new underlying for the same product.

Now (2): yes, it can be a security token, in two ways

Option A · quickest
Tokenize the lender side

An SPV pools the loans and their collars (the collared-lending vault) and funds itself by issuing eWpG crypto securities (tokenized notes) to lenders. The lender's position is now a security token: it can be sold before maturity, pledged, and tranched (§2). Senior slices go into the ISIN note (§3). The client side stays a normal loan.

Option B · full product
The whole ZiC as a security

Modelled on the prepaid variable forward. The client transfers the asset to the SPV and receives cash now, plus a security token for the residual: the asset value at maturity, held between floor and cap, minus the loan. Lenders hold the senior notes. Both sides are now tradeable: the client could even sell the residual token before maturity.

Recommendation: start with A, since it is standard note issuance and doesn't change anything for the client, and build B once tax and legal are clear.

Watch-outs (verify with Legal/Tax)Under option B, moving the asset to the SPV may count as a sale for tax purposes, which for many clients removes the point of borrowing instead of selling. Prospectus exemptions are needed (qualified investors only, or €100k minimum denomination), and so is a KWG view on whether the SPV is doing lending business. Remaining risks for lenders: the option counterparty, custody, execution at maturity, basis (when hedging with a near substitute) and a liquidity mismatch between lenders and loan terms.

Target clients

  • End clients: HNW individuals and founders with concentrated positions, companies holding BTC treasuries, family offices.
  • Distributors: crypto exchanges and neobrokers (white-label), and private banks that want to offer collar loans on crypto and tokenized assets.
  • Lenders / note buyers: credit funds, crypto-native funds, and treasuries via the senior note (§3).

5Prediction-market baskets

Decision (Max, 17 Nov 2026)The baskets contain dozens of individually auditable legs, and we market-make them ourselves. Liquidity and market making are not the constraint.

Market & demand

Companies and investors are exposed to events: tariffs, sanctions, export controls, elections, an AI slowdown. Prediction markets are growing fast and now price thousands of these events. The demand to hedge with them sits mostly with institutions that can't access them.

Why it is inefficient today

  • Insurance: political-risk and trade-credit insurance is slow, bespoke and excludes a lot, and payout needs a claims process.
  • Single prediction bets: all-or-nothing on one wording, scattered across venues, and not holdable for a bank or corporate.

Now: a basket in one security

A basket bundles 10–30 binaries pointing at one theme. Each leg has a weight, and the basket pays the weighted sum of the legs that resolve "yes". Fair price = Σ weight × probability, taken live from where each leg trades.

Example: "AI slowdown" basket (illustrative) 8 legs, e.g. GPU rental prices fall, hyperscalers cut capex, a top AI lab raises at a lower valuation, new chip-export rules, the semiconductor index falls more than 25%. Price ~25.65 per 100. If 3 legs of 15% each come true, it pays 45. If the whole theme plays out, the payout approaches 100. If nothing happens, the buyer loses the premium, like an insurance premium.
  • Smoother than single bets: it pays partly when the theme partly happens. One leg resolving on a technicality only moves it by that leg's weight.
  • Auditable: every leg has a fixed resolution source, date and rule, and an independent calculation agent confirms outcomes.
  • Packaged: one ISIN, one price, one line on the statement. We quote from live leg prices and hedge on the venues or in our own book.
Sizing an exporter's hedge A machine builder sells €20m a year into a region. In an escalation scenario they lose ~€6m. If a "regional escalation" basket would pay ~60 per 100 in that scenario, they need ~€10m notional. At a price of 8 that costs €800k for €6m of protection, a number the CFO can compare with trade-credit insurance.

Target clients

  • Exporting SMEs and corporates: via their house bank, the way they already buy FX hedges.
  • Private banks & wealth managers: thematic certificates for their product shelf, white-labelled.
  • Macro and hedge funds: defined-loss exposure to a theme.
  • Banks (own book): hedge concentration in the loan book, e.g. heavy lending to exporters into one region.
Watch-outs (verify with Legal)In the EU, binaries are derivatives. The ESMA/BaFin product-intervention rules on binary options may apply to retail distribution, so we need to check whether a prospectus-backed basket note falls inside them. Institutional sales fall under MiFID II and, if OTC, EMIR. For IFRS 9 hedge accounting, the buyer must document the economic link.
